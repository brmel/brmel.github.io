How Big Tech Posts on LinkedIn

1. The Weekly Rhythm: When Feeds Wake Up

I tracked 2,923 posts from 99 Big Tech companies across 31 time zones, timed to the millisecond. Here are the companies' own 2,585 posts, by weekday and head-office hour. The busiest window in global tech is a tie: Tuesday and Wednesday at 2 p.m., with 48 posts each.

The Peak WindowTue and Wed, 2:00 PM

The busiest local hours in global tech, tied at 48 posts.

The Office Hours Rule54% Workday

Posted 9 a.m. to 5 p.m., Monday to Friday. Weekends are just 6%.

The Mid-Week RidgeTue to Thu (63%)

Nearly two-thirds of all weekly volume concentrates here.

Do the Busiest Hours Earn More?

Not really. Posting when everyone else posts earns about the same as posting at any other time. By day, a post runs from 0.89 on Saturday to 1.11 on Tuesday. By part of the day, it only moves from 0.97 to 1.02.

2. How Often to Post: The Audience Fatigue Curve

Does posting every day give you more reach? The data says no: companies posting under 3 times a week earn about 5× more engagement per follower than companies posting 12 times or more. Impressions are private to each page, so engagement (reactions, comments and reposts) stands in for reach. Every company below is its own median, on posts at least 72 hours old.

The Honest Takeaway: more posts did not mean more engagement per follower. These are different companies, so this can't prove that posting less would raise your own numbers. But nothing here says that posting more would.

The Best Rate: About 2 to 3 Posts a Week

Cutting the same companies into narrower groups shows where the peak sits. Companies posting 2 to 3 times a week earn 0.42 per 1,000 followers, including Amazon, OpenAI, Meta and Anthropic. Below 1 post a week and above 12, it falls to 0.04 and 0.03.

Pages of the Same Size Show the Same Pattern

Small pages get more engagement per follower, so size could explain the curve. Comparing pages of a similar size, the selective group stays at or near the top, and pages posting 12 or more times a week sit lowest wherever they exist.

Page Size Matters Even More

This number follows the size of the page more closely than anything else. Its rank correlation is −0.78 with followers and −0.32 with posts per week. A page with ten times the followers only gets about 2 times the reactions.

Is It Really Fatigue?

If posts ate into each other, a post published soon after the previous one should do worse. It doesn't: a post under 3 hours after the last one earns 1.01 times the company's usual. So whatever lowers the heavy broadcasters, it doesn't show up from one post to the next.

Local Hour (Head-Office Clock)

When marketing teams configure their publishing schedule. The day peaks twice, in the morning and again in the afternoon, with the highest hour at 3:00 PM (213 posts).

UTC Hour (Global World Clock)

When the worldwide LinkedIn feed fills up simultaneously. The same posts on one shared clock peak at 14:00 UTC (234 posts), when the European afternoon overlaps the North American morning.

3. The Automation Habit: Why So Much Drops at :00

20% of all posts went live exactly at :00, and 29% on a quarter-hour (:00, :15, :30, :45), against 2% and 7% if the minutes were random. A round minute looks like a scheduling tool, though I can't see which tool a company uses.

It's the habit of a few pages: 7 companies published 51% of the :00 posts, and 27 of the 81 companies with at least 10 posts never used it. A post at :00 earns 0.97 times the company's usual.

4. What Actually Works: 5 Lessons From 2,585 Posts

Simple, practical lessons for any company wanting to grow without burning out its audience. This is one month of observation, not an experiment, so read them as what the data suggests.

Rule 01

Post 2 to 4 Times a Week, Not Twice a Day

Pages posting 12 or more times a week earn 80% less engagement per follower than pages posting under 3 times, and the peak sits at 2 to 3 a week.

Rule 02

Don't Worry About :00 (Everyone Schedules on the Hour)

20% of posts go out at exactly :00, and those posts earn 0.97 times the company's usual. Scheduling on a round minute costs almost nothing, so schedule when it suits you.

Rule 03

Photo Carousels Beat Video

Several images in a swipeable post get double the engagement of video (0.11 vs 0.05 per 1,000 followers). Inside one company the gap is smaller, 1.35 against 1.00, but it points the same way.

Rule 04

Share Real Milestones, Skip Corporate Awards

Posts labelled as partnerships, products or corporate news earn 0.09 to 0.14 per 1,000 followers, against 0.03 for awards: about 3× more. There are only 26 award posts, and the labels are rough.

Rule 05

Share What You're Building, Don't Spam Product Pitches

About 11% of posts are labelled as selling something. When a page does promote a product, it earns 0.09 against 0.06, so the odd product post doesn't hurt. Most of the feed still isn't a pitch.

5. What Formats Stop the Scroll?

Video is the most common format at 37%, but several images earn the most among the common formats, at 0.11. Polls and events earn the least.

The Same Formats, Inside One Company

Page size can flatter a format in the chart above, so here each post is measured against its own company's usual post. On that measure, several images earn 1.35, video 1.00 and document carousels 0.75.

What Topics Get Shared?

Primary subject matter across all posts, ranked by publishing share. Customer stories and thought leadership fill most of the feed, while corporate news is rare and earns the most.

Selling vs. Storytelling (And the Companies That Post Nothing)

About 11% of posts promote a product, and only 2% announce a new launch. Apple and Tesla posted nothing at all on their main page this month.
